# Story, psychology and rhythm

Why people keep watching, and how to structure a video so they do. This is the layer above
motion: the best-animated frame in the world does nothing if the piece has no spine.

---

## 1. The first three seconds

The hook lands within **1–3 seconds** and does three jobs at once:

1. **Break the pattern** — something visually or verbally unexpected, because brains are wired
   to filter patterns and to stop at whatever breaks one.
2. **Set a promise** — an explicit expectation of what the viewer gets.
3. **Open a gap** — curiosity or tension the brain compulsively wants closed.

**The numbers:** per Meta's internal data, 65% of viewers who watch the first three seconds
watch at least ten, and 45% reach thirty. Videos holding above ~65% at three seconds see
several times the impressions of ones that don't. Almost all of a video's distribution is
decided in its first three seconds — which means the hook deserves more authoring effort than
any other beat, and usually gets less.

### Hook strategies

| Strategy | Use when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Shocking statistic | a credible number quantifies the stakes | "90% of plastic ever made has never been recycled." |
| Rhetorical question | you want an immediate cognitive gap | "Why does time speed up as you get older?" |
| Counterintuitive claim | the truth contradicts common belief | "Adding lanes to a highway makes traffic worse." |
| Pain validation | the audience already feels the confusion | "Everyone says 'just diversify' — nobody says what that means." |
| Visceral metaphor | the idea is abstract and needs a body | "Your attention is a spotlight, and apps fight over the switch." |
| Concept announcement | the term itself is the subject | "There's a word for this: the bystander effect." |
| Direct address | the audience is sharply defined | "If you've ever rage-quit a recipe halfway — this is for you." |
| Imagine / scenario | a thought experiment frames the piece | "Imagine money that loses value if you don't spend it." |
| Stakes / consequence | the "why now" is a real cost | "Get this one step wrong and the whole batch is ruined." |

**Never open with a generic definition, a title card, or a logo.** Nothing has been promised,
so there is no reason to stay.

The structural frame the short-form literature converges on is **hook → retain → reward**: earn
the stop, sustain with continuous small payoffs, and deliver something at the end that
justifies the time.

## 2. The core rule of adaptation

> **An article is an information dump. A video is a guided act of understanding.**

Do not follow the source's paragraph order. Reorder, merge, omit and compress into a teaching
sequence. **The most common failure is paraphrasing the source in order.** The input is the
source of *information*, not a story template.

Before writing a single beat, extract:

- **Audience** — who this speaks to, and what they already don't know.
- **Gap / stakes** — the confusion or "why care" the piece resolves.
- **Thesis** — the one line the viewer should walk away with.
- **Spine** — the 3–6 ideas that build to the thesis.
- **Evidence** — the concrete numbers, examples, comparisons that ground it.
- **Landing** — the takeaway, or the call to think / try / act.

The thesis should land **by beat 2**. Everything after it is its evidence.

---

## 3. Pick one structure

Do not splice phases from different structures — each is a complete path through understanding.

| Structure | It is… | Use when the payload is… | Body shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| **concept-explainer** | "what is X, and why it matters" | one idea the audience half-knows | name the concept → reveal the mechanism layer by layer → land the implication |
| **how-to-process** | "here is how X works," ordered | a procedure with a clear start→finish | 3–6 steps on one consistent stage, one move each |
| **listicle** | "N things about X" | parallel, co-equal items | hook → N items → wrap; rule of three is strongest |
| **story-explainer** | teach through narrative | case studies, histories, cautionary tales | setup → tension → turn → resolution → lesson |

Compounds layer an outer arc with an inner rhythm ("concept-explainer with process"). Name the
compound explicitly; a process inner rhythm implies tighter seams and shorter beats.

**The body is a sequence, not a frame.** An explainer's core is 3–6 body beats on a *consistent
visual stage*, each advancing one step or layer, building cumulatively. A single isolated body
beat teaches nothing.

---

## 4. Give every beat a job

Each beat needs one clear job. Beats that only say "more detail" or "another point" should be
cut. Per beat, name:

- **role** — hook · pain/problem · name-the-idea · mechanism/step/item · implication ·
  evidence · thesis/landing · call to act.
- **persuasion / clarity technique** — a *named* technique, never "explain the idea."
- **emotional beat** — a specific feeling, never "positive" or "interested."
- **scene** — one-line visual idea.
- **voiceover** — or explicitly empty.
- **seam** — how it hands off to the next beat.

### Clarity technique catalog

| Family | Techniques |
|---|---|
| **Make-concrete** | analogy / metaphor · concretisation (abstract → tangible) · worked example with real numbers · anchoring on a familiar referent |
| **Reveal-in-order** | progressive disclosure · build-up (simple → general) · signposting ("first… then… finally") |
| **Contrast** | before/after · belief vs reality · two options compared · counterexample |
| **Structure** | rule of three · numbered enumeration · question→answer pairing · frame-then-fill |
| **Evidence** | statistical proof · citation · demonstration (show the mechanism running) · causal chain A→B→C |
| **Memory & landing** | callback to the hook's image · distillation to one line · coined term / mnemonic · generalisation |

### Emotional arc

An explainer rides a comprehension arc, and the arc should have real variation:

- **Negative valley** — *open the gap*: curiosity · puzzlement · surprise · tension · scepticism
- **Pivot** — *orient*: clarity · orientation · anticipation · focus
- **Build** — *build understanding*: comprehension · "aha" · confidence · momentum · conviction
- **Resolution** — *land*: satisfaction · resolve · inspiration · inevitability

Compound beats are usually strongest: "surprise + recognition," "comprehension + delight."

## 5. Rhythm and pacing

**Pace** is how fast the piece progresses; **rhythm** is the pattern of cuts, transitions and
internal movement.

- **Name the rhythm before implementing.** *fast-fast-SLOW-fast-SHADER-hold.* Declare which
  beats are quick hits, which are holds, where energy peaks.
- **Derive it from the content and the brand, not from the runtime.** A 15-second ad for an
  architecture studio and a 15-second ad for a gaming brand are the same length and should not
  have the same rhythm.
- **Energy should peak where the narration lands its heaviest emphasis.**
- **Do not overstuff.** A 15-second video that hooks, shows three features and ends on a CTA
  reads as noise. "Hook–hold–CTA" with one strong feature is often the correct rhythm for 15
  seconds.
- **Cut at the natural end of an action or sentence.** Cutting before an action completes feels
  unfinished; holding too long after loses momentum.
- **Vary the beat length deliberately** — uniform beat lengths are the pacing equivalent of
  using one easing curve.

### Timing to music

- Compute the bar. At 111 BPM one bar = 2.143 s; a 2/2/1/2/2/1-bar structure is 21.429 s.
- **Land reveals ON the beat, don't start them on one.** An entrance of duration `d` that
  should settle on beat `b` starts at `b − d`. Passing `b` as the start makes it arrive `d`
  late and nothing is actually synced.
- **Don't beat-snap everything.** Sequential text has a reading floor of ~1.1 s per line that
  outranks the grid.

---

## 6. Narration

- **The narration must not read the screen.** The picture carries the numbers; the voice
  carries the reasoning. If the voice says what the frame says, cut one.
- **1–2 sentences per spoken beat**, usually 6–20 words.
- **Write in discrete cues, not one run-on breath.** "First the snowball — then the hill — then
  the speed" hands the shot its reveal cadence for free. A single long clause leaves the frame
  nothing to pace to. This is what makes the sequential-reveal doctrine (§02) implementable.
- **Concrete and human.** Strong: "Compound interest isn't addition, it's a snowball — every
  turn picks up the snow from the last, then more." Weak: "The study, published in 2019,
  examined three cohorts and found that…" — that is reading, not explaining.
- **Silent beats are allowed and common** — a diagram assembling, a worked example animating, a
  held moment before a turn. When a beat is silent, its visual has to carry what the script
  doesn't say.
- Avoid: "unlock the power of," "seamless experience," long noun-phrase lists, and any beat
  that is only a filler bridge.

---

## 7. Continuity across beats

Two levers make a run of separate beats read as one continuous piece:

1. **A consistent stage** — consecutive body beats share the same composition idea: the same
   diagram growing, the same number line, the same desk.
2. **A consistent seam** — pick one transition type for the run and repeat it. Ordered steps →
   a consistent directional push. A soft layer reveal → crossfade. Pick 2–3 seam types for the
   whole video and reuse them; a different transition at every seam is as monotonous as one
   transition at every seam, in the opposite direction.

When a single element genuinely *transforms* between two ideas (a diagram node becomes a chart
bar, a formula becomes its result), keep it **inside one beat** as a development move rather
than splitting it across a seam.
