# *How a loaf of bread cost 200 billion marks* — how it was made

A 78.3 s portrait short about the German hyperinflation of 1923: twenty-five archival stills cut
to a Kokoro narration under a fixed headline, with red caption chips. Structure copied from a
reference reel the user supplied (a 50 s piece on Soros and the Bank of England), measured at
720×1280 and scaled to 1080×1920.

**Nothing here is the render.** MP4s are gitignored in this repo — they are build output. What
lands is the source: the script, the picked images, and the two scripts that build the timeline.

## Reproducing it

```bash
bay --mcp                       # or open the app; both serve 7333
python3 narrate.py              # phase A: create the project, speak the 25 lines
python3 visuals.py              # phase B: cut the picture to the voice
```

`hunt.py` re-runs the image search and rewrites `bank.json`; `picks.json` is the choice made from
its output by eye. `narrate.py` must run first and must run *whole* — it creates the project, so
running `visuals.py` twice against one project doubles every element.

**Narration comes first on purpose.** Guessing caption durations and fitting speech to them gets
both wrong. Speaking first means every chip start, every cut and every marker is a measured
number rather than an estimate.

## Where the pictures came from

All twenty-five were found with `stock_search` against Wikimedia Commons and re-ranked by
`bay-vision` (SigLIP) against a description of what the frame should *show*. `sources.json`
records the stock id, title, licence and pixel dimensions of each; `CREDITS.md` is the
attribution, generated by `stock_credits`.

Two things about the search are worth knowing before changing the queries:

- **The archive and the encoder want opposite inputs.** Commons matches filenames and wikitext,
  so it wants `"hyperinflation 1923"` and returns *nothing at all* for a sentence. SigLIP matches
  what is in the frame and gets better the longer and more concrete the description. Hence the
  separate `query` and `describe` fields, and hence the `q`/`d` pair on every entry in
  `script.json`'s sibling `beats.json`.
- **The good material is ShareAlike.** The Bundesarchiv released its Weimar photographs as
  CC BY-SA 3.0 de, which `stock_search` refuses by default. Every search here passed
  `licence: "allow-sharealike"`, and the consequence is that **the finished video is CC BY-SA**.
  See `CREDITS.md`.

The pictures are cover-cropped to the 1080×1122 panel by ffmpeg before import, so no element
needs a `crop_set` — that is a one-shot state change, and pre-cutting keeps the resampling under
our control rather than the compositor's.

## What the gate made us do

`check` returns `status: findings`, `skipped: []`, with exactly one warning (below). Three
structural decisions came out of reading the rules rather than from taste, and all three are
also just how an edit works:

1. **Every shot moves twice.** `held()` samples a whole beat and *the last sample wins*, so an
   entrance that has settled reads identically at the end of its own beat and at the end of the
   next one, and carries nothing however visible it was. Each shot therefore takes an entrance on
   its own beat and a `shunt` on the seam, which leaves it standing somewhere new.
2. **The seam move has no opacity channel.** `press`, `swell` and `z-in` are all at opacity 0 on
   their first frame, so an element using one is not even on stage at its own beat. `shunt` keeps
   the picture visible while it is pushed away, and the incoming fade lets you watch it go — the
   carry has to be *seen*, not merely declared.
3. **A bed had to be authored.** Without one, `speech-masked` and `bed-too-quiet` both come back
   skipped, and a suite that could not run has not passed. There is no music pack to install, so
   `bed.wav` is generated with ffmpeg: filtered brown noise plus two slow low tones. Room tone,
   not a tune. It is gitignored with the other generated audio; the command is in `visuals.py`'s
   header.

## Measured

| | this | round-2 references | wicks-v2 (the condemned one) |
|---|---|---|---|
| duration | 78.3 s | 30.0 / 46.2 s | — |
| integrated loudness | **−14.8 LUFS** | −16.0 | −13.9 |
| loudness range | 1.8 LU | 3.6 | — |
| mean frame delta | **0.67 %** | 2.73 / 3.13 % | 0.57 % |
| `check` errors | 0 | — | — |

Two of these are honest failures rather than wins:

- **Frame delta is barely above the video this engine was rebuilt to prevent.** The cause is real
  and not fixable by tuning: these are *photographs*. The reference reel this copies uses moving
  archival footage, which is most of why it feels alive. A `drift` pan was added to every shot
  and moved the number from 0.57 % to 0.67 % — chasing it further would be optimising the metric
  rather than the film, which is precisely the round-1 mistake.
- **Loudness range 1.8 LU is flat**, worse than round 2's already-poor 3.6. Continuous narration
  over a constant bed has almost no dynamics. Level is now on target; range is still unsolved.

## The one remaining warning

```
off-vocabulary-motion — bedclip: volume is hand-keyed rather than a named move (2 segments)
```

This is unavoidable and is a defect in bay, not in the piece. `duck` is the tool whose entire job
is keyframing bed volume, and `volume` has no entry in the move registry — so **any** use of
`duck` reports an off-vocabulary channel. Either the registry needs a volume move or the motion
audit should exempt the channel; until then the warning is noise on every ducked mix.
